## Editorial overview

This work combines Thoreau’s extended experiment at Walden Pond—an account of living simply in nature, philosophical reflections on economy, solitude, knowledge, and moral law—with his separate essay on the moral duty to resist unjust government. The Walden chapters move from practical rules of economy and the reasons for isolation, through close seasonal and natural observation that supplies metaphor and evidence for his ethical claims, to explicit moral and metaphysical arguments (Higher Laws) and a concluding restatement of the experiment’s outcomes. 'On the Duty of Civil Disobedience' is an argumentative treatise that supplies an applied moral and political corollary to the individualist ethics developed in Walden.

**Central question:** How should an individual best live—economically, morally, and spiritually—in relation to society and nature, and what is the correct response of conscience when government is unjust?

**Governing thesis:** A deliberate life simplified to essentials—rooted in self-reliance, close observation of nature, and obedience to one's inner moral law—yields truer freedom, insight, and virtue than pursuit of conventional wealth, social status, or blind conformity; when government violates justice, individuals have a moral duty to resist through nonviolent civil disobedience.
